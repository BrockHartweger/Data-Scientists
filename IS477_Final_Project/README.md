In addition to offensive statistics, there are many identifiable variables such as player ID, game ID (gid), team abbreviation, opponent team, and game date. ## Data Cleaning (Retrosheet + Lahman MLB Datasets)

The data cleaning process for both the Retrosheet and Lahman datasets was necessary to address missing values, formatting concerns, and irrelevant variables. The Retrosheet dataset is too large and is structured at the game level; it requires extensive preprocessing and cleaning to ensure usability for aggregation and modeling.


For the Retrosheet dataset, the first step was to remove any unnamed or automatically generated variables that were not a part of the original dataset. Such variables were not informative for the scope of this project, and were just acting as unnecessary noise. The next step dealt with the missing game identifiers of which were removed as these data points could not be reliably linked to any specific players. With the statistics, a substantial amount of incomplete rows were seen, particularly in observations lacking any statistical values at all. After filtering these invalid records, the dataset was again altered by removing rows where players had zero at bats. These observations carried no substance to our presentation because our group was focused on offensive production. This step reduced noise and showed only players who had an offensive at bat.

Next, the dataset contained a large number of missing values across certain variables, such as hits, home runs, RBIs, and strikeouts. Rather than removing all incomplete rows, these missing values were filled in with the number zero, saving thousands of rows. In addition, several nonmeaningful redundant columns were removed, such as eject, php, and box, to improve efficiency, simplicity, and data structure as a whole. A new variable for year was created by extracting the first of the four digits from the date variable. This allowed the dataset to be easily grouped together into separate eras: early, mid, and modern. This allows for comparative analysis later on in the project for visualizations, model, and comparative analysis. After cleaning the Retrosheet dataset, the number of observations reduced from 5.7 million rows to around a respectable 4 million rows, split up among the three eras. 
​

For the Lahman team dataset, cleaning was less intensive due to its pre-structured formality. The pre-structured format was unlike Retrosheet’s in the way the data was already in season level format. The missing values were mainly in the categorical variables, such as division identifiers and award indicators. These were filled with ‘Unknown’ or ‘N’ identifiers, which needed to be filled with the number zero where appropriate. Other numerical variables needed to be filled with zero, such as attendance, stolen bases, and numerous offensive batting metrics. No rows were removed from this dataset, avoiding mass loss of data. 
​

Overall, the cleaning process ensured both datasets were error-free, had consistent formatting, and were suitable for aggregation and modeling. The Retrosheet dataset required substantial overhauling and transformation due to its structure (game-like vs. season-like). The Lahman dataset required only minor changes, such as filling in missing values and removing non-essential variables. These steps were essential in preparing the data for the visualizations, classifier model, and showcasing long-term trend analysis.

## Findings
Upon exploring and analyzing the MLB batting and team data from 1898-2020, we identified clear patterns at both the league and player level. A visualization of historical league data showcased several major trends. Firstly, batting averages varied significantly across different points in tim, ranging from 0.23 all the way to 0.29. They peaked during the 1920-30s but then dropped sharply in the late 1960s during a time when pitching was heavy. They do recover slightly from the 1980s to 2000s but once again drop closer to 2020. Total home attendance has increased dramatically from 2–5 million annually in the early 1900s to the high 70–80 millions annually in the early 2000. Obviously, we do see a sharp drop in 2020 because of the Corona virus. For fielding errors, they have decreased a lot since the 1900s, going from over 5000 yearly to a 2500–3000 range yearly. This shows long term improvements in defense. 

The linear regression model was developed to estimate the individual player home runs per season using features like at-bats, hits, RBIs, walks, and strikeouts. This model performed quite well, obtaining a train R^2 of 0.8466 and test R^2 of 0.8424. The test RMSE is also 3.44 home runs, showcasing consistent performance. We saw that the predicted values for players hitting home runs closely matched the actual values, especially in the 5–35 home runs range. However, for extreme performances that went over 50 home runs, the model did appear to underestimate in its predictions. Through the coefficients, we see that RBIs has the strongest positive impact on predictions, which makes sense because it is primarily used to measure a batter’s ability to produce runs. On the other hand, doubles and triples were negatively associated with home-runs, which also checks out because teams with high triples tend to follow a speed-oriented strategy, whereas teams with high home runs are more power forward. 

The Random Forest classification model was coded to predict whether a player would hit at least 10 home runs in a season, thus allowing to differentiate between regular hitters and power hitters. A stratified 80/20 split was used on players with at least 100 at-bats which is basically a statistic representing a batter’s turn. This model achieved a high accuracy of 90% on a test set with 6374 samples. For players with less than 10 home runs, the precision was 0.95 and the recall was 0.91, and for players with more than 10 home runs, precision and recall values were 0.81 and 0.89 respectively. This shows strong overall model performance. Also, the model’s AUC ROC score of 0.967 is extremely strong and indicates an excellent ability to differentiate between classes. I think adding balanced class weights helped acknowledge the class imbalance without impacting the model’s performance on the majority group 


## Future Work
Since the current analysis only focuses on batting patterns and predicting home run performance, there are a ton of ways to expand on it. For starters, the batting data we are considering can be made richer by adding pitching and fielding data, to get a more 360 degree view of performance. Variables like opponent Earned Run Average (ERA) which is the average number of earned runs a pitcher allows, strikeout rates, and pitcher handedness can add more context to a hitter’s result. This is because having a 0.28 batting average against a strong pitcher holds different weight than achieving the same average with a weaker pitcher. However, to integrate these datasets, it would require meticulous alignment across games which can be very tricky, thus making it out of this project’s scope. 

Another option is to enhance feature engineering and add more context to a player’s performance over raw statistics. We can consider features like park factors, which adjust for how different stadiums influence outcomes differently, or rolling average, which is a player’s average home runs or batting performance across several seasons. Park factors can identify if a player’s totals are inflated because they play more often in stadiums where scoring home runs is easier. Rolling average can capture when a player is improving, declining or just performing consistently which is important for roster forming. Including these features can allow for a more nuanced analysis as it is grounded in stronger context and the model’s performance can be much stronger. 

Alternatively, the linear regression model can be refined, even though it performed well, because it did struggle to predict extreme outcomes like seasons having over 50 home runs. This does show some nonlinearity in the relationships within the data. For added complexity, more advanced methods like gradient boosting can be used to capture any effects of the nonlinearity. For the Random Forest classifier, we can consider incorporating techniques like cross-validated grid search to make it near perfect and produce even more reliable results. 
Another way to expand the project is broadening the target itself, so that we are not just limiting our analysis to home runs but other metrics. These can include metrics like Wins Above Replacement (WAR) which measures a players overall values to their team  or On-base Plus Slugging Plus (OPS+) which measures a batter’s total offensive value. These measures could produce an even more nuanced view of players and team value. 

Since our dataset spans from 1898 to 2020, which is a wide range, and we can imagine that over this time period baseball has evolved significantly, it could be creating several models with data across a shorter span of time. These eras could be divided into sections like 1898-1950, 1951-1990, and 1991-2020, and we can then compare the results to see how performance drivers have changed over time. Separate models would definitely produce stronger results than one single model for such a vast time range. 

Lastly, we can improve the data’s coverage, as there appeared to be gaps in the data in the earlier year, as well extreme outliers for 2020 given the global pandemic. In the future, missing data can be filled in by referencing other historical sources. Then, any outliers can be excluded for a richer and more consistent performance. 


## Challenges
One of the issues that came up was dealing with a dataset that consisted of 5.7 million rows, distributed across over 21000 individual datasets, one per player, which needed to be combined into a single dataset. The file that resulted from this came out to be 565MB. This created performance issues because loading a file of this size took extremely long and was memory intensive. To mitigate this, the data was sliced into smaller datasets, each with about 500000 rows and was processed with tools like glob and pd.concat. After doing this, the preprocessing stage became more efficient. Given this storage challenge, in the future, it could be worth identifying a more efficient storage solution that further reduces load time and can work on target queries rather than needing the full dataset to load. 

Another issue we faced was coordinating work and simultaneously working on the code because of all the data taking up ~1.5GB. We could not upload the data to GitHub because it can’t take more than 25-50MB and it could not even be sent over email, so we resorted to working on the code on one person’s laptop until the very end. Once we uploaded the data to Box and generated a shareable link, the other person could access and download the exact files needed for the code to run and make edits on their end, making the code a bit more efficient. 

Inconsistencies in the data was another challenge that we faced, because the data spanned a really vast range of over a century. Data from the earlier years was missing fields, did not follow the same format, and might not reflect any changed definitions of statistics. Specifically, pre-1950 data is missing values in the intentional walks section as it was not noted consistently. In these situations, it is important to carefully interpret the data and treat it like 0 rather than actual missing data to make sure historical accuracy has been preserved. It would also need to be cleaned by someone with great familiarity with baseball. 

Also, we saw that the Random Forest classification model had a class imbalance issue where most player-seasons were under the 10 home run threshold, while higher home run seasons were pretty rare. This needs addressing because the model could then produce high accuracy by simply producing the majority class which is misleading. Therefore, to address this, the model included class_weight = ‘balanced’ to account for the uneven distribution we see. Because of this, the model was able to achieve strong overall accuracy and a strong recall of 0.89 for the minority class, which shows that the model is not biased.
